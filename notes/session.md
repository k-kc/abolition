Beccaria main: from CHAPTER XII. OF THE INTENT OF PUNISHMENTS.
"From the foregoing considerations it is evident, that the intent of punishments is not to
torment a sensible being, nor to undo a crime already committed. Is it possible that
torments, and useless cruelty, the instruments of furious fanaticism, or of impotency
of tyrants, can be authorized by a political body? which, so far from being influenced
by passion, should be the cool moderator of the passions of individuals. Can the
groans of a tortured wretch recal the time past, or reverse the crime he has committed?
The end of punishment, therefore, is no other, than to prevent others from committing
the like offence. S**uch punishments, therefore, and such a mode of inflicting them,
ought to be chosen, as will make strongest and most lasting impressions on the minds
of others, with the least torment to the body of the criminal.**"

Beccaria alt: from Chapter II : the right to punish 
"The multiplication of mankind, though slow, being too great for the means which the
earth, in its natural state, offered to satisfy necessities, which every day became more
numerous, obliged men to separate again, and form new societies. These naturally
opposed the first, and a state of war was transferred from individuals to nations.
Thus it was necessity that forced men to give up a part of their liberty; it is certain,
then, that every individual would chuse to put into the public stock the smallest
portion possible; as much only as was sufficient to engage others to defend it. The
aggregate of these, the smallest portions possible, forms the right of punishing: all that
extends beyond this is abuse, not justice."

Beccaria alt: from Chapter VI: OF THE PROPORTION BETWEEN CRIMES AND
PUNISHMENTS 

"That force which continually impels us to our own private interest, like gravity, acts

Against the death penalty
- It is not the intenseness of the pain that has the greatest effect on the mind, but its
continuance; for our sensibility is more easily and more powerfully affected by weak,
but by repeated impressions, than by a violent but momentary impulse.
- The execution of a criminal is, to the multitude, a spectacle which in some excites
compassion mixed with indignation. These sentiments occupy the mind much more
than that salutary terror which the laws endeavour to inspire.
- There are many who can look upon death with intrepidity and firmness; some through fanaticism, and others through vanity,
which attends us even to the grave; others from a desperate resolution, either to get rid
of their misery, or cease to live: but fanaticism and vanity forsake the criminal in
slavery, in chains and fetters, in an iron cage; and despair seems rather the beginning
than the end of their misery
- There is also another advantage in the punishment of slavery, which is,
that it is more terrible to the spectator than to the sufferer himself; for the spectator
considers the sum of all his wretched moments, whilst the sufferer, by the misery of
the present, is prevented from thinking of the future. All evils are increased by the
imagination, and the sufferer finds resources and consolation, of which the spectators
are ignoran; who judge by their own sensibility of what passes in a mind by habit
grown callous to misfortune
- He reasons thus: “What are these laws that I am bound to
respect, which make so great a difference between me and the rich man? He refuses
me the farthing I ask of him, and excuses himself by bidding me have recourse to
labour, with which he is unacquainted. Who made these laws? The rich and the great,
who never deigned to visit the miserable hut of the poor; who have never seen him
dividing a piece of mouldy bread, amidst the cries of his famished children, and the
tears of his wife. Let us break those ties, fatal to the greatest part of mankind, and only
useful to a few indolent tyrants. Let us attack injustice at its source. I will return to my
natural state of independence. I shall live free and happy on the fruits of my courage
and industry. A day of pain and repentance may come, but it will be short; and for an
hour of grief, I shall enjoy years of pleasure and liberty. King of a small number, as
determined as myself, I will correct the mistakes of fortune; and shall see those
tyrants grow pale and tremble at the sight of him, whom, with insulting pride, they
would not suffer to rank with dogs and horses.”

## Small-group activities
1. Defend/Critique (15 minutes)
How it works: Split the room into two groups. One group's job is to make the strongest possible case for an argument or position from the text. The other group's job is to identify the philosophical problems with it. They don't have to believe what they're arguing. Then they present their cases to each other.
Why it works: Forces students to think charitably about difficult positions before dismissing them. Gets at the philosophical stakes. Works especially well with texts you're reading critically (Bentham, carceral logic, etc.).
Example prompt: "Group 1: Make Bentham's utilitarian argument as compelling as possible on its own terms. Group 2: What's the fundamental philosophical problem with his logic? Present to each other."
Time: 10 min prep/discussion in groups, 5 min presentations and quick response

2. Rhetorical Analysis Pairs (12-15 minutes)
How it works: Pair students up. Give them a specific passage. One person's job: explain what the author is doing rhetorically in this passage (how they're building an argument, what moves they're making). Other person's job: identify what philosophical or political work that rhetorical move accomplishes. Then they switch and do it on a different passage.
Why it works: Teaches students to notice craft and strategy in texts. Connects form to philosophical content. Quiet enough that introverts can participate. Creates accountability (you have to explain it to one person, not the whole room).
Example prompt: "Turn to the person next to you. Person A: Explain how Douglass builds credibility in this passage—what rhetorical choices does he make? Person B: What philosophical or political argument does that credibility-building serve? Then switch roles on this other passage."
Time: 7 min per passage, with a quick 1-2 min debrief with full group

3. Text in Conversation (15-18 minutes)
How it works: Small groups (3-4 people). Give them two texts or passages side by side. Their job: identify one key similarity and one key difference in how the authors are approaching the same or related problem. Then they present what they found.
Why it works: Teaches comparative thinking and synthesis. Helps students see philosophical lineages and interventions. Forces them to articulate what they notice rather than just passively reading.
Example prompt: "In your group, compare how Douglass and Jacobs each use testimony as a political argument. What's similar about their rhetorical strategy? What's different? What problem is each one trying to solve?"
Time: 10-12 min group work, 3-5 min presentations

4. Problem-Posing (12-15 minutes)
How it works: Groups of 3-4. Give them a concept or argument from the text. Their job isn't to analyze it—it's to ask good questions about it. What's confusing? What doesn't follow? What would you need to understand better? What assumptions is it making? They generate 3-5 real questions, not rhetorical ones.
Why it works: Legitimizes confusion and uncertainty as intellectual work. Helps you see what students are actually struggling with. Produces genuinely interesting questions for full-group discussion. Takes pressure off having to have answers figured out.
Example prompt: "Your group's job is not to explain the panopticon. It's to generate the best questions you can about how Bentham's logic actually works. What's genuinely confusing? What would you need to understand better to accept or reject his argument? Come up with 3-5 real questions."
Time: 10 min generating questions, 3-5 min sharing (you pick the most interesting ones for full group)

5. Historical/Contemporary Application (15-18 minutes)
How it works: Groups of 3-4. Give them a historical moment or contemporary case. Their job: apply the philosophical framework from the text to that moment. What does this text help you see about X? What does it miss or get wrong?
Why it works: Keeps theory grounded. Helps students see relevance. Produces concrete thinking rather than abstract theorizing. Makes the intellectual work feel consequential.
Example prompt: "Your group is thinking about contemporary surveillance technology—facial recognition, predictive policing, digital monitoring. Using Bentham's logic about the panopticon, what does he help us understand about these systems? What important things about them does his framework miss?"
Time: 10-12 min group work, 3-5 min presentations or report-back

6. Genealogy Trace (12-15 minutes)
How it works: Groups of 3-4. Give them a key concept or claim from the text. Their job: work backwards. Where does this idea come from? What earlier thinker or tradition is the author drawing on, responding to, or rejecting? Then: how has this concept been used differently in other contexts or by other people we've read?
They're not summarizing positions. They're tracing an idea's history through the texts and conversations you've been studying.
Why it works: Teaches students to see ideas as having genealogies—they don't appear out of nowhere. Helps them understand intellectual interventions (what makes this version of the idea different?). Creates real synthesis across the semester. Mirrors the actual work of political theory scholarship.

## writing prompts
1. Clarification Write (5 minutes)How it works: "Write down one thing from today's text that you genuinely don't understand. Not 'I'm confused about the whole thing,' but be specific: What sentence or idea or logical move doesn't track for you?"Why it works: Gets at actual confusion rather than performative engagement. Gives you insight into what to address. Students feel permission to not have it all figured out. Produces better questions than you'd generate on your own.When to use it: After introducing a difficult passage or complex argument. Early in the seminar when you're checking comprehension.

2. 2. Stakes Clarification (5 minutes)
How it works: "In 2-3 sentences: Why does this argument matter? What would be different if the author were right versus wrong?"
Why it works: Forces students to articulate why they should care about what they're reading. Separates "this is what the text says" from "this is what's at stake." Helps you see if students understand the consequence of an argument.
When to use it: After you've done textual analysis and want to move toward philosophical significance. Mid-seminar check.

3. Disagreement Write (5 minutes)
How it works: "Write one thing the author says that you genuinely disagree with or find troubling. Be specific about what and why."
Why it works: Activates critical thinking. Gives students permission to push back. Produces the kind of engaged disagreement that's valuable in political theory. Often reveals assumptions students didn't know they had.
When to use it: After you've spent time understanding the argument on its own terms. You want them to have understood it before they critique it.

4. Connection Write (5-7 minutes)
How it works: "How does something in today's text connect to or challenge something we've read before? Be specific about which text and what the connection is."
Why it works: Synthetic work. Helps students build intellectual coherence across the semester. Reveals what they're actually retaining and thinking about. Produces material for comparative discussion.
When to use it: Mid-to-late semester when you have enough texts to compare. After a break, to reconnect to earlier material.

5. Question Generation Write (5 minutes)
How it works: "Write one genuine question you have about this text. Not rhetorical, not a challenge—a real question you want answered or explored more."
Why it works: Different from problem-posing because it's individual first. Students often have questions they're embarrassed to ask in full group. This legitimizes curiosity. You get better questions than discussion generates. It's low-risk.
When to use it: When you sense students are holding back or when discussion has stalled. Early in the semester to build trust.

6. Assumption Excavation (5-7 minutes)
How it works: "Write: What is the author assuming about [human nature / democracy / justice / political action / etc.] in order for this argument to work? What would have to be true for their logic to hold?"
Why it works: Teaches students to read beneath arguments to their foundations. Develops critical thinking about philosophical commitments. Especially powerful for political theory because it reveals how different thinkers start from different premises.
When to use it: When you want students to understand the deep structure of an argument rather than just its surface claims. Works well after you've already discussed what the argument is.
