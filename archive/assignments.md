# Assignments & Rubrics

[← Back to Home](index.html)

## Overview

This course centers on developing your capacity to think critically about abolitionist political thought and to produce original scholarly analysis. All assignments build toward the final seminar paper, giving you structured opportunities to develop your research question, engage with scholarly literature, and refine your argument.

---

## Grade Breakdown

* **Weekly Response Papers** (Weeks 2-5): 20%
* **Topic Proposal** (Week 6): 10%
* **Annotated Bibliography** (Week 8-9): 15%
* **Extended Proposal/Prospectus** (Week 11): 15%
* **Full Draft** (Week 13): 10%
* **Final Seminar Paper** (Week 15): 30%

---

## Assignment Details

### Weekly Response Papers (Weeks 2-5)

**Length:** 2 pages (double-spaced)  
**Due:** Before each class session

These short papers give you space to engage critically with weekly readings and begin identifying themes or questions that interest you. Rather than summary, focus on analysis: What claims does the author make? What evidence or reasoning supports those claims? What questions or tensions emerge in the text?

As you write these responses, pay attention to what draws your interest—these papers will help you identify a viable topic for your seminar paper.

**What makes a strong response paper:**
- Engages directly with specific passages or arguments from the reading
- Raises substantive questions rather than simply agreeing or disagreeing
- Makes connections between readings or identifies tensions within a single text
- Shows evidence of careful, close reading

### Topic Proposal (Week 6)

**Length:** 2-3 pages (double-spaced)  
**Due:** [DATE]

After encountering different abolitionist traditions, carceral critiques, and contemporary movements, you will propose 2-3 possible research topics. For each potential topic, provide:

* A preliminary research question or area of inquiry
* Brief explanation (1 paragraph) of why this topic interests you
* Initial thoughts about which texts/thinkers you might engage
* Any concerns or questions you have about feasibility

I will provide feedback to help you select the strongest topic and refine your approach.

---

### Annotated Bibliography (Weeks 8-9)

**Length:** 6-8 sources with annotations  
**Due:** [DATE]

Once you have selected your topic, this assignment asks you to identify and engage deeply with relevant scholarship. Your annotated bibliography should include:

**Sources:**
- At least 2 primary sources (writings by abolitionists, activists, or thinkers central to your topic)
- At least 4 secondary sources (scholarly articles or book chapters analyzing your topic)

**Annotations (150-200 words each) should:**
- Summarize the main argument or contribution of the source
- Explain how this source relates to your research question
- Note specific insights, methods, or frameworks that inform your thinking
- Identify how this source connects to other sources in your bibliography

This assignment demonstrates that you can locate relevant scholarship and engage with it seriously. Quality matters more than quantity—choose sources that genuinely illuminate your topic.

---

### Extended Proposal/Prospectus (Week 11)

**Length:** 5-6 pages (double-spaced)  
**Due:** [DATE]

This is your roadmap for the final paper. You should present:

**1. Research Question (1 page)**
- What question drives your paper?
- Why does this question matter for understanding abolitionist thought?
- What specific puzzle, tension, or debate are you addressing?

**2. Theoretical Framework & Literature Review (2-3 pages)**
- What scholarly conversations does your paper engage?
- What concepts or frameworks will guide your analysis?
- How does your intervention differ from or build on existing scholarship?

**3. Argument & Structure (1-2 pages)**
- What is your central argument or thesis?
- How will you develop this argument? (Provide a section-by-section outline)
- What evidence will you use?

This is where I provide substantive feedback on the viability and direction of your project. A strong prospectus makes the final paper much easier to write.

---

### Full Draft Workshop (Week 13)

**Length:** 12-15 pages minimum (complete draft)  
**Due:** [DATE]

Submit a complete draft of your seminar paper for peer review. This should be a genuine draft—not a rough outline or collection of notes—with introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion.

You will exchange drafts with a peer and provide written feedback using the provided rubric. This process helps you:
- Identify weaknesses in your argument before the final submission
- Practice giving constructive scholarly feedback
- See how other students approached similar challenges

**Peer Review Guidelines:**
- Is the argument clear and well-supported?
- Does the paper engage seriously with primary and secondary sources?
- Are there sections that need development or clarification?
- What are the paper's greatest strengths?

---

### Final Seminar Paper (Week 15)

**Length:** 15-20 pages (double-spaced)  
**Due:** [DATE]

Your final paper should present an original argument about abolitionist political thought, grounded in careful engagement with primary texts and relevant scholarship.

**Strong papers will:**
- Develop a clear, focused argument in conversation with scholarly debates
- Demonstrate sophisticated engagement with primary sources
- Use secondary literature effectively (not just citing, but building on or challenging existing interpretations)
- Situate abolitionist thinkers in their historical and political contexts
- Write with clarity and precision

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Argument (40%):** Clear thesis, logical development, original insights
- **Evidence (30%):** Effective use of primary sources, engagement with scholarship
- **Analysis (20%):** Critical thinking, attention to complexity, theoretical sophistication
- **Writing (10%):** Organization, clarity, proper citation (Chicago style)
